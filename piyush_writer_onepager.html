Building a parking detection system that actually had to work

+

Most object-detection demos are shown off in good lighting, from one fixed camera angle. The parking lot we built ours for gave us none of that — shifting shadows through the day, cars parked closer together than any textbook example, and a live feed that occasionally just froze for a second.

We still got the model to 99.5% precision, largely by training on our own footage instead of trusting a generic dataset to cover it. But the part that took the most time wasn't the detection itself — it was making sure two people couldn't book the same spot at once, since the model updates the second a car actually leaves.

That's usually how these projects go. The "AI" part gets the attention, and the plumbing around it quietly takes twice as long to get right.

Self-written practice piece — technical project write-up

What I learned automating something I used to do by hand

+

For a while, I used to read through a handful of business newspapers every morning just to catch startup funding news. It worked, but it ate an hour I didn't have.

So I built a small pipeline instead — it scrapes ten newspapers a day, pulls out anything that looks like funding news, and uses an AI model to turn the messy article text into a clean summary: company name, amount raised, who's behind it. It lands in my inbox every morning as a short digest.

The scraping part was easy. The hard part was teaching the system what actually counted as "worth including" — the same judgment call I used to make in my head without noticing I was making it. That's usually where these projects get interesting.

Self-written practice piece — first-person, founder-style

What a messy spreadsheet was hiding

+

The retail dataset I was working with had the usual problems — missing review ratings, inconsistent column names, a duplicate field nobody remembered adding. None of that is interesting on its own; it's just the part of the job that has to happen before anything else can.

Once it was cleaned up and sitting in a proper database, the actual question was simple: did subscribers spend differently from one-time customers? Turned out they did, and by more than I expected — but only once you looked at spend alongside shipping method and discount usage together, not on their own.

It's a small reminder that the cleaning step isn't busywork. It's usually where you decide whether you'll even be able to see the answer later.
